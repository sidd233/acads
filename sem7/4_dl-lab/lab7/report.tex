\part*{Robust Aggregation of Per Sample Squared Errors}

\section{Objective}

\begin{itemize}
\item Train a fixed convolutional network on MNIST with three different aggregations of the per sample squared error, namely the mean, the median and the ten percent trimmed mean, under one identical optimization protocol.
\item Measure how the choice of aggregator changes convergence speed, final clean accuracy, and the degradation caused by label noise and by input noise.
\item Quantify the sensitivity of each aggregator to a small number of grossly inflated squared errors inside a mini batch.
\item Determine how many samples of a mini batch actually contribute to the parameter gradient under each aggregator, and in particular whether \texttt{backward()} produces a usable gradient for the median.
\item Establish how the three aggregators respond to a change of batch size, and propose a research hypothesis from the observed behaviour.
\end{itemize}

\section{Theory}

\subsection{The three aggregators}

Let a mini batch contain $n$ samples. For sample $i$ let $y_i \in \{0,1\}^{10}$ be the one hot target and $\hat{y}_i = \sigma(z_i) \in (0,1)^{10}$ the sigmoid output of the network. The per sample squared error is the squared Euclidean distance between the two vectors,
\begin{equation}
s_i = \sum_{c=1}^{10} (y_{ic} - \hat{y}_{ic})^2 .
\end{equation}
The three losses differ only in how the vector $s = (s_1, \dots, s_n)$ is reduced to a scalar:
\begin{align}
L_{\mathrm{MSE}} &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} s_i , \\
L_{\mathrm{MedSE}} &= \mathrm{median}(s_1, \dots, s_n) , \\
L_{\mathrm{T\text{-}MSE}} &= \frac{1}{n-k}\sum_{j=1}^{n-k} s_{(j)}, \qquad k = \lfloor 0.1 n \rfloor ,
\end{align}
where $s_{(1)} \le \dots \le s_{(n)}$ is the sorted batch and the $k$ largest entries are discarded. For $n = 64$ this trims $k = 6$ samples and keeps $58$.

\subsection{Breakdown point and influence}

Each aggregator is a statistic of the empirical distribution of $s$, so it inherits a breakdown point, that is the smallest fraction of arbitrarily corrupted entries that can drive the statistic to an arbitrary value. The mean has breakdown point $0$, since a single entry sent to infinity sends the mean to infinity. The median has breakdown point $1/2$. The one sided $10\%$ trimmed mean has breakdown point $0.1$, because once more than $k$ entries are corrupted at least one of them survives the trim.

This is the exact prediction tested in Experiment 4. Inflating $m$ of the $n$ squared errors by a factor $\alpha$ changes the mean by
\begin{equation}
\frac{\Delta L_{\mathrm{MSE}}}{L_{\mathrm{MSE}}} = (\alpha - 1)\,\frac{\sum_{i \in \mathcal{O}} s_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} s_i} ,
\label{eq:mseshift}
\end{equation}
which for $\alpha = 100$, $m = 2$ and roughly uniform errors is about $99 \times 2/64 \approx 309\%$. The median and the trimmed mean are affected only through a change of rank, not through the magnitude of the corruption.

\subsection{Gradients of a selection}

The gradient with respect to the parameters is obtained by the chain rule through the weights $w_i = \partial L / \partial s_i$:
\begin{equation}
\nabla_\theta L = \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i \, \nabla_\theta s_i .
\end{equation}
For the mean, $w_i = 1/n$ for every $i$, so all $n$ samples contribute. For the trimmed mean, $w_i = 1/(n-k)$ on the kept samples and $w_i = 0$ on the trimmed ones. The median is a selection operator: with $n$ even, \texttt{torch.median} returns the lower of the two central order statistics, which is a single element of the batch. It is therefore differentiable almost everywhere, with
\begin{equation}
w_i = \mathbb{1}\!\left[ i = i_{\mathrm{med}} \right] ,
\end{equation}
so \texttt{backward()} does produce a gradient, but that gradient is exactly the gradient of one sample and carries no information about the other $n-1$.

This has a direct consequence for gradient magnitude. If the per sample gradients $\nabla_\theta s_i$ are roughly uncorrelated with comparable norm $g$, then averaging gives $\|\nabla_\theta L_{\mathrm{MSE}}\|_2 \approx g/\sqrt{n}$, whereas the median gives $\|\nabla_\theta L_{\mathrm{MedSE}}\|_2 \approx g$. The median therefore produces larger and far noisier steps, and its step norm does not shrink as the batch grows.

\subsection{Effective sample usage}

A further consequence is that the median consumes exactly one sample per optimizer step regardless of $n$. Over $E$ epochs on $N$ training samples with batch size $n$ there are $EN/n$ steps, so the median run applies gradient information from only $EN/n$ sample presentations while the mean uses all $EN$. The median is thus predicted to lose accuracy in proportion to the batch size, which is what Experiment 6 measures.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\begin{itemize}
\item Dataset: MNIST loaded with \texttt{torchvision.datasets.MNIST}. A fixed permutation with seed 42 selects 10000 training images and 2000 test images. The test split is never corrupted in any experiment.
\item Preprocessing: images are kept in $[0,1]$ while noise is injected, then normalized with mean $0.1307$ and standard deviation $0.3081$. Injecting the Gaussian noise before normalization keeps the prescribed standard deviation of $0.1$ in the pixel domain where it is meaningful.
\item Architecture: $\mathrm{Conv}(1 \to 8, 3\times3) \to \mathrm{ReLU} \to \mathrm{MaxPool}(2) \to \mathrm{Conv}(8 \to 16, 3\times3) \to \mathrm{ReLU} \to \mathrm{MaxPool}(2) \to \mathrm{FC}(400 \to 10)$, followed by a sigmoid on the ten outputs. The spatial sizes are $28 \to 26 \to 13 \to 11 \to 5$, giving $16 \times 5 \times 5 = 400$ features into the fully connected layer.
\item Optimization: plain SGD with learning rate $0.01$, no momentum, no weight decay, batch size 64, 5 epochs, seed 42. The seed is reset immediately before every model is constructed and the data loader generator is seeded identically, so every run in the report starts from bit identical weights and sees the same batch order. Only Experiment 6 changes the batch size.
\item Targets: one hot vectors of length 10 compared against the sigmoid outputs, with no softmax and no cross entropy anywhere.
\item Label noise: a seeded subset of the training labels is replaced by $(y + u) \bmod 10$ with $u$ drawn uniformly from $\{1, \dots, 9\}$, which guarantees the new label is wrong.
\item Input noise: a seeded subset of the training images receives additive $\mathcal{N}(0, 0.1^2)$ noise per pixel. Labels are untouched and the values are not clipped.
\item Reported test loss uses the same aggregator as training, evaluated per batch of 64 on the clean test set and averaged over batches.
\end{itemize}

\section{Algorithm}

\begin{enumerate}
\item Select the 10000 training and 2000 test samples with the seeded permutation and hold them as tensors in memory.
\item For each loss aggregator, reset the seed to 42, build the CNN, and train for 5 epochs with SGD. In every step compute $s_i$ for the batch, reduce it with the chosen aggregator, call \texttt{backward()}, read $\|\nabla_\theta L\|_2$ over all parameters before the update, and then step.
\item At the end of each epoch evaluate the loss and the accuracy on the clean test set and record the epoch wise history.
\item Repeat the three runs with $10\%$ and $20\%$ of the training labels corrupted, and separately with $10\%$ and $20\%$ of the training images corrupted, comparing the final test accuracy against the clean baseline.
\item For the outlier study, take the first batch of 64 at initialization, compute $s$, multiply the first two entries by 100, and record the percentage change of each aggregator.
\item For the gradient study, differentiate each aggregator with respect to $s$ itself in order to count the entries with $\partial L/\partial s_i \neq 0$, then differentiate with respect to $\theta$ to obtain the norm. Verify the median claim by comparing $\nabla_\theta L_{\mathrm{MedSE}}$ against $\nabla_\theta s_{i_{\mathrm{med}}}$ directly.
\item Repeat the clean training with batch sizes 16, 64 and 128 and compare the final accuracy and the mean gradient norm.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Implementation}

The entire difference between the three experiments is contained in the two functions below.

\begin{lstlisting}
def squared_errors(outputs, labels):
    """Per sample squared error s_i = sum_c (y_ic - yhat_ic)^2 on one hot targets."""
    targets = torch.zeros_like(outputs)
    targets.scatter_(1, labels.unsqueeze(1), 1.0)
    return ((targets - outputs) ** 2).sum(dim=1)


def aggregate(errors, kind):
    if kind == "MSE":
        return errors.mean()
    if kind == "MedSE":
        return errors.median()
    if kind == "T-MSE":
        count = errors.numel()
        keep = count - int(TRIM_FRACTION * count)
        ordered, _ = torch.sort(errors)
        return ordered[:keep].mean()
    raise ValueError(kind)
\end{lstlisting}

The count of samples that actually reach the parameters is obtained by backpropagating into a detached copy of the error vector, so that $\partial L / \partial s_i$ is materialised explicitly.

\begin{lstlisting}
retained = errors.clone().detach().requires_grad_(True)
aggregate(retained, kind).backward()
weights = retained.grad
contributing = int((weights != 0).sum().item())
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Analysis}

\subsection{Clean data costs the robust aggregators accuracy}

On clean data the ordering is MSE at $85.70\%$, T-MSE at $80.70\%$ and MedSE at $53.85\%$. MSE crosses $80\%$ test accuracy at epoch 3 and T-MSE only at epoch 5, while MedSE never reaches it. This is the expected price of robustness under a clean distribution. The largest squared errors on clean MNIST belong to genuinely hard but correctly labelled digits, so discarding them discards signal. Trimming $10\%$ costs about 5 percentage points at a fixed budget of 5 epochs, and the median, which effectively discards $63$ of every $64$ samples, costs about 32 points.

The MedSE curve in Figure 1 is also visibly non monotone, falling from $47.45\%$ at epoch 2 to $41.25\%$ at epoch 3 before recovering. Since each update is the gradient of a single sample, the update direction has the full variance of an individual example and the trajectory is correspondingly unstable.

\subsection{Trimming pays for itself only when the tail is corrupted}

The noise experiments invert the clean ordering. Under $20\%$ label noise MSE falls by $0.95$ points to $84.75\%$ while T-MSE rises by $4.30$ points to $85.00\%$ and therefore overtakes MSE. The mechanism is exactly the one predicted by the breakdown argument: a mislabeled sample produces a large squared error because the network's sigmoid output agrees with the true class and disagrees with the corrupted target, so mislabeled samples concentrate in the upper tail of $s$ and are precisely what the trim removes. At $20\%$ corruption the trim is only $10\%$ wide, so it removes roughly half of the mislabeled samples, which is enough to recover the gap. The same effect is visible in the training accuracy on the corrupted set, where MSE reaches $68.17\%$ against T-MSE at $68.63\%$ while both generalize to around $85\%$, that is, neither has begun to memorize the wrong labels within 5 epochs.

Input noise of standard deviation $0.1$ leaves MSE and T-MSE essentially unchanged, within $0.1$ points at both levels. A perturbation of this size on normalized MNIST behaves as mild data augmentation rather than as an outlier source, and it does not move the affected samples into the tail of the error distribution, so there is nothing for a robust aggregator to reject. This is a useful negative control: robust aggregation helps against corrupted targets, not against mildly corrupted inputs.

The MedSE column of the noise table should be read with care. Its apparent gains of $6.70$ to $10.50$ points are measured from a baseline that is far from converged, and the input noise response is non monotone, rising to $64.35\%$ at $10\%$ corruption and returning to $53.90\%$ at $20\%$. These are run to run fluctuations of an unstable optimizer rather than evidence of robustness.

\subsection{Outlier sensitivity follows the breakdown point exactly}

Two inflated samples out of 64 raise MSE by $294.25\%$, which matches Equation \ref{eq:mseshift} once the actual share of those two samples in the batch error, about $3.0\%$, is substituted. The median moves by $0.26\%$ and the trimmed mean by $0.45\%$. Neither moves by the magnitude of the corruption, only by the rank displacement it causes. For the trimmed mean the residual $0.45\%$ has a precise cause: the two inflated samples were not originally among the six largest, so inflating them pushes them into the trimmed set and pushes two previously trimmed samples back into the retained average, which changes the retained mean slightly. Since $2/64 = 3.1\%$ is well below the $10\%$ breakdown point, the corruption itself is fully rejected.

\subsection{The median does produce gradients, but from one sample}

\texttt{backward()} succeeds for MedSE. PyTorch treats \texttt{median} as a selection and routes the entire incoming gradient to the selected index, so the count of entries with $\partial L/\partial s_i \neq 0$ is $1$ out of $64$, against $58$ out of $64$ for T-MSE and $64$ out of $64$ for MSE. The claim was verified directly rather than inferred: the median sample of the batch was \input{tables/median_fact}\unskip, that is, the two gradients are bitwise identical. The gradient is therefore affected by only the median loss sample and not by the complete batch.

The norms confirm the variance argument of Section 2.3. MedSE produces $\|\nabla_\theta L\|_2 = 8.045$ against $4.211$ for MSE on the same batch and from the same weights, because MSE averages 64 partially cancelling per sample gradients while MedSE returns one of them undiluted. A larger gradient norm here signals higher variance, not faster learning, which is consistent with MedSE being both the noisiest and the slowest of the three.

\subsection{Batch size separates the aggregators by an order of magnitude}

At a fixed budget of 5 epochs, increasing the batch size reduces the number of optimizer steps from $3125$ to $785$ to $395$, and all three losses degrade: MSE falls from $94.70\%$ to $85.70\%$ to $79.60\%$. The median falls much further in relative terms, from $73.85\%$ to $53.85\%$ to $53.10\%$, and this is the predicted effect of Section 2.4. A median run applies gradient information from one sample per step, so across the whole run it uses $3125$, $785$ and $395$ sample presentations respectively, against $50000$ for MSE at every batch size. Its effective data efficiency is $1/n$.

The averaged gradient norms make the same point from the other side. For MSE the mean norm falls from $2.515$ to $1.598$ to $1.000$ as the batch grows, close to the $1/\sqrt{n}$ behaviour expected from averaging uncorrelated per sample gradients. For MedSE it does not fall at all, standing at $3.233$, $5.319$ and $5.089$, because no averaging takes place. Larger batches therefore buy the mean a better conditioned gradient and buy the median nothing except a proportionally smaller fraction of the data.

\section{Research Hypothesis}

Two findings sit next to each other in the tables and suggest the same remedy. Trimming helps exactly when the discarded tail is corrupted, costing $5.0$ points on clean data and gaining $4.3$ points at $20\%$ label noise, and the median throws away the batch average in order to buy a breakdown point it does not need at $3\%$ contamination.

\textbf{Hypothesis.} \emph{A trimmed aggregator whose trim fraction is matched to the actual contamination rate, and whose trimming is switched on only after the per sample error distribution separates into two modes, will dominate both MSE and a fixed $10\%$ trimmed mean at every noise level, matching MSE on clean data and exceeding it under label noise.}

The reasoning is that early in training all squared errors are large and a clean hard sample is statistically indistinguishable from a mislabeled one, so an early trim removes informative examples at random; only once the network has fitted the clean majority do the mislabeled samples separate into a distinct upper mode. The contamination rate can be estimated online from the bimodality of the batch error histogram, which makes the scheme self tuning rather than another hyperparameter.

A second and independent hypothesis follows from the gradient results. \emph{Replacing the median by the mean of the central half of the batch, that is the interquartile mean, will retain the median's rejection of inflated errors while restoring the $1/\sqrt{n}$ gradient variance of the mean, and will therefore close most of the 32 point accuracy gap between MedSE and MSE without reintroducing MSE's zero breakdown point.} This is testable within the present setup by changing one line of \texttt{aggregate}, and the predicted signature is a mean gradient norm that falls with batch size in the way MSE's does rather than staying flat in the way MedSE's does.

\section{Conclusion}

Under one fixed protocol the three aggregators separate cleanly along the robustness and optimization trade off. The mean is the fastest and most accurate on clean data at $85.70\%$ but has breakdown point zero, and two inflated samples in a batch of 64 move its value by $294\%$. The $10\%$ trimmed mean gives up $5.0$ points on clean data in exchange for a $10\%$ breakdown point, rejects the same outliers to within $0.45\%$, uses $58$ of every $64$ samples for its gradient, and overtakes the mean once $20\%$ of the labels are wrong. The median is robust for the wrong reason: its breakdown point of $50\%$ is far more than the problem requires, and it is paid for by using one sample per update, which leaves it at $53.85\%$, non monotone in the epoch curve, and degrading in direct proportion to batch size. For the corruption levels studied here the trimmed mean is the only aggregator that is robust and trainable at the same time, and the practical conclusion is that the trim fraction, not the choice of aggregator family, is the quantity that should be tuned to the data.
